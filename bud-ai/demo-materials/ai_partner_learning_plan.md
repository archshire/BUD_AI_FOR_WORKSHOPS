# AI Partner Onboarding — Learning Plan / Curriculum
**Companion to:** `facilitator_onboarding_script.md`
**Audience:** Workshop participants (learners), delivered live by the facilitator at session start
**Format per chapter:** Chapter title → Learning objectives → Comprehension check

---

## Chapter 1 — Why We're Here: Translation Isn't the Same as Understanding

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- State, in their own words, why translated words alone don't guarantee shared understanding.
- Identify at least one situation from their own experience where a correct translation still led to a misunderstanding.
- Describe what the AI Partner is *for*, at a high level, before learning any of its specific features.

**Comprehension check**
1. In one sentence, what's the difference between "translating a sentence" and "understanding what someone meant"?
2. True or false: if the AI Partner gives you a perfect translation, that means you've understood the instruction. (Answer: False — explain why.)
3. Open question: Can you think of a time a message was translated correctly but still confused you? What was actually missing?

---

## Chapter 2 — ME: Your Private AI Partner

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- Explain that each participant has a private AI Partner distinct from the shared workshop space.
- Describe how the AI checks in ("Quick check — where are you at?") instead of declaring someone confused.
- Demonstrate giving an honest response to a check-in (including "I'm fine," "I'm lost," or "I disagree").
- State that they can correct the AI, and that the AI will accept and drop a resolved concern.

**Comprehension check**
1. If your AI Partner checks in on you, what are three different kinds of honest answers you could give?
2. What should happen if you tell the AI "no, I've got this" after it checks in? What should NOT happen afterward?
3. Scenario: You say "I disagree with this approach," and the AI treats that as a sign you're confused and need help. Is that correct behavior? Why or why not?
4. True or false: your private conversation with your AI Partner is automatically visible to the facilitator. (Answer: False — explain what actually happens instead, covered fully in Chapter 5.)

---

## Chapter 3 — US: When Translation Isn't Enough Between Two People

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- Describe a situation where a peer's translated message still leaves room for misread meaning.
- Demonstrate how to ask their own AI Partner "what did they mean by that?"
- Explain that the AI's answer is a *hedged interpretation*, not a certain fact about what the other person meant.
- State what happens if they ask the AI to help confirm meaning with the original speaker.

**Comprehension check**
1. You get a translated message from a teammate, but something about it feels off. What's your next step?
2. When the AI offers an interpretation of what someone meant, should you treat that as confirmed fact? Why or why not?
3. Scenario: Two participants actually understood each other correctly but simply disagree about the answer. Should the AI treat this as a "misunderstanding" that needs fixing? What should happen instead?
4. Who has the final say on what a person meant by what they said — the AI, or the person themselves?

---

## Chapter 4 — THE ROOM: How the Facilitator Sees the Workshop

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- Describe, at a high level, what the facilitator can see about the room (patterns, signals, recommendations).
- Explain that the facilitator does not see a raw transcript of private ME/US conversations by default.
- State that the facilitator makes the final call on any recommendation the AI surfaces.

**Comprehension check**
1. What kind of information does the facilitator receive about you by default — raw chat logs, or something else? What is that "something else"?
2. If the AI notices a pattern across several participants, does it act on its own, or does someone else decide what happens next? Who?
3. True or false: the AI can override the facilitator's decision if it disagrees. (Answer: False.)

---

## Chapter 5 — Privacy and Trust: What Stays Private

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- State the boundary between public/shared workshop space and private participant–AI space.
- Explain that raw private content requires the participant's explicit permission before the facilitator can see it.
- Describe what happens if a participant refuses a permission request (i.e., that "no" is final).

**Comprehension check**
1. Name the two spaces described in this chapter (public/shared vs. private participant–AI space).
2. If the facilitator wants to see your private conversation with your AI Partner, what has to happen first?
3. Scenario: You refuse a permission request from the facilitator. What happens next — does the system find another way to get that information, or does it stop there?
4. Is the privacy boundary something the AI has simply been *asked* to respect, or is it enforced structurally by the app? Why does that distinction matter?

---

## Chapter 6 — Putting It Into Practice: Norms for This Session

**Learning objectives**
By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:
- List the practical norms for participating in the workshop (language choice, honest check-in answers, disputing translations, escalating to the facilitator directly).
- Demonstrate readiness to begin the workshop task using the AI Partner appropriately.

**Comprehension check**
1. What should you do if a translation feels wrong to you?
2. What should you do if something isn't working and the AI Partner doesn't resolve it?
3. Quick self-check: can you restate, in one sentence each, what ME, US, and THE ROOM are responsible for?

---

## Facilitator note (not shown to participants)

This curriculum maps directly onto the onboarding script's four content sections (ME, US, THE ROOM, Privacy) plus a framing chapter and a practical-norms close. If time is short, Chapters 1 and 6 can be compressed into a single opening/closing minute each — Chapters 2–5 carry the substantive commitments (agency, correction, hedged interpretation, facilitator boundary, and consent) and shouldn't be cut.

Comprehension checks are designed to be askable **out loud, informally**, in the room — not as a written quiz — to confirm participants actually absorbed the boundary conditions (especially: disagreement ≠ confusion, and refusal is final) before the live task begins.
